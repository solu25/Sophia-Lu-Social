00 · The product

Pay rent using crypto, earn rewards

MegPrime Pay is a rewards program built around the biggest recurring payments people make: rent, mortgage, and eventually home purchases.

The closest comparison is Bilt Rewards. Bilt lets you pay rent with a credit card and earn points. MegPrime is broader. You pay rent and mortgage using MP tokens, the company’s own crypto, and earn cashback-style rewards every month at rates from 2 percent up to 20 percent at partner properties.

What happens underneath
Bank→MegPrime account→MP tokens→Dollars→Landlord

The reward lands 30 days later. The user never has to think about any of it. They see “pay rent, earn rewards.”

The team was racing toward a super-MVP launch. Everything had to support shipping fast, not perfectly.

The product overview page that introduces the rewards program to a first-time visitor.

01 · The design problem

Five places a user might back out

When you use Venmo, you already know how it works. Pick a person, enter an amount, tap send. Same with Bilt: connect a credit card, set your rent amount, done.

MegPrime is different because money moves through more steps before it reaches the landlord. Each of those steps is a place a user might pause, get nervous, or back out.

The user we were designing for is not a crypto enthusiast looking for a new toy. She is a renter or homeowner who saw a 10 percent reward and immediately thought:

2% on standard rent, up to 20% at partner properties, mortgage rebates and home-purchase rewards. The same screen that draws her in is the one that makes her ask what the catch is.

This sounds too good to be true. What’s the catch?

How do you onboard a skeptical user into a brand new payment model, at MVP speed, without making her learn anything complicated before she sees the value?

That question shaped every decision over the next three months.

02 · The foundation

What I built before designing a single screen

The team had a business model and a Jira backlog, but no shared design foundation. No persona, no principles, no decision framework. Without one, every design conversation could be re-litigated by the loudest voice in the room. Under an MVP timeline that was not affordable, so I built it first.

The primary persona

The Value Optimizer. When she sees a 10 percent cashback offer on her rent, her first thought is suspicion.

“Who We’re Building For” — the persona designed to receive 100 percent of design attention.

Two Jobs to be Done

Users hire the product to do a job. Understand the job, not just the feature request.

Job 01
Pay my bills without thinking
Functional

Landlord gets exactly $2,500, on time, every month. I get confirmation.

Emotional

Feel responsible, avoid late-fee stress, not worry whether the “crypto thing” will work.

Social

Maintain a good tenant reputation. Don’t look foolish if a payment fails.

Job 02
Maximize value from an unavoidable expense
Functional

Earn $250 to $500 a month in rewards. Track annual savings. Know it’s real money.

Emotional

Feel smart for finding this. A sense of beating the system, legally.

Social

Tell friends. Be the person who found the life hack.

Job 1 has to be solved before Job 2 can sell. If she is afraid her rent will fail, no reward will get her to try. The onboarding had to lead with reliability, not rewards.

03 · Decision rules

Six principles, so the team argued principles and not opinions

When there is no precedent to borrow, the only thing holding the work together is a set of rules everyone agrees to. These were not decoration on a slide. They were the operating system for every screen, every flow and every disagreement.

Principle 01
Bill Pay First, Crypto Second

Users come to pay bills. Crypto is the rails, not the destination. USD-first interface, always.

Principle 02
Familiar Beats Novel

Match Venmo and Zelle patterns. Introduce crypto concepts only after the core task succeeds.

Principle 03
Clarity Over Surprise

High rewards trigger skepticism. Over-communicate fees, timing and constraints, upfront.

Principle 04
Reliability Over Features

One payment type that works beats five with bugs. Ship slow, earn trust.

Principle 05
Optimize for the Second Payment

The first payment proves it works. The second proves we’ve earned the habit.

Principle 06
Constraints Are Product

NYC and CA exclusions, $5K limits. Surface them early, explain why, set expectations.

04 · Competitor research

What to borrow, and what to build new

I focused on Bilt Rewards, MegPrime’s closest analog. The point was not to copy it. It was to work out what to borrow and what to build new.

Reverse-engineering Bilt’s three BillPay paths revealed the mechanic: a virtual ACH pass-through that instantly charges the user’s credit card when the landlord’s portal pulls. The account is always empty.

Borrowed

The virtual account pattern. She adds MegPrime’s routing and account number to her landlord’s portal once, and from there the portal pulls automatically.

Built new

Bilt’s instant settlement does not work on crypto rails. MegPrime’s conversion chain takes days, so funds have to sit in the account before the portal pulls.

That timing gap drove three design responses
  • A funding deadline.
  • An “always target next month” rule.
  • A safety net: the old payment method stays active until MegPrime is fully funded.

Three BillPay paths mapped at both the user-facing and backend levels. The virtual-account pattern was borrowable; the instant settlement was not.

05 · The screens

Every decision tied to a principle

The bill-pay onboarding had to take a skeptical user from “what’s the catch?” to a scheduled payment, without asking her to learn how crypto works. Every step ties back to one of the six principles.

1
Value prop and reward calculation

The first screen leads with the reward, not account creation. She picks her property type (owner, renter, partner property), each showing the reward rate up front, then calculates her own reward against her actual rent.

Principle · Optimize for the Second Payment

The calculator turns an abstract promise into a personal number before any commitment. Leading with her own reward number is the cheapest possible first trust deposit.

The flow opens with the reward, not the sign-up. Property type sets eligibility up front, and the calculator turns an abstract promise into a personal one.

2
Gather bill data

Bills look simple from the outside. In practice she could be paying a small landlord or a large management company, through a portal, cash, check, Zelle or Venmo. Each combination produces a different path for getting the data we need.

Principle · Familiar Beats Novel

I mapped the decision space before designing the screens. She does not see the matrix. She sees a three-step add-a-bill flow. The complexity lives in the logic underneath.

The decision matrix mapping how users actually pay their bills today, and the simple add-a-bill flow that came out of it.

3.1
Add mortgage bill

Mortgage setup runs four steps: property address, lender info, bank details and payment schedule, with a final review. The review screen carries the most weight.

Principle · Clarity Over Surprise

Every detail is shown back to her, including the full fee structure (swap fee, conversion fee, who pays what) and the reward projection. Hidden fees are what made her suspicious in the first place. Visible fees are how she stops being suspicious.

Four steps to set up a mortgage. The review screen reveals the full fee structure and reward projection before she commits.

3.2
Add rent

Rent setup uses the same flow shape as mortgage: address, payee, bank details, schedule, review. The eligibility logic and the recipient differ, but the path she walks is identical.

Principle · Reliability Over Features

One flow that handles both bills correctly beats two parallel flows that drift apart. She is using one product, not two.

Rent setup mirrors mortgage, deliberately.

4
The ACH path

For users whose landlords do not use a payment portal, MegPrime needs the landlord’s bank details to push payment directly. Most people have never had to ask their landlord for that information.

Principle · Constraints Are Product

So the flow opens with a “How it works” explainer, and scenario states handle the edge cases: insufficient MP balance, unlinked bank, auto-fund toggle. The harder path is acknowledged and designed for, not hidden.

The “How it works” screen opens the flow so she understands what she is being asked to do before she does it.

06 · Outcome

What shipped, and what I would test next

Three months produced a strategic foundation, a designed onboarding flow for rent and mortgage, an alternate ACH path, and the documentation for engineering and product to pick up after the engagement closed.

What did not happen was launch. The contract ended before the product reached real users, so there are no conversion metrics, retention numbers or NPS scores to point to. This is about the work itself, not the outcome.

I would rather show real work without manufactured results than borrow numbers I cannot defend.

The three tests I would run first
QuestionHow I would answer it
Does the value-prop-first opening actually convert skeptics, or do they want the catch explained before they will touch the calculator?Five-user usability tests.
Is the 30-day reward delay tolerable, or does the gap between paying and earning erode trust before month two?Prototype test with a pending-rewards dashboard.
Does the ACH path’s “How it works” screen do enough, or do users still abandon when asked to gather landlord bank details?Funnel measurement on explainer-to-completion drop.

These are the questions I left on the table. They are also the ones I would want to be asking on day one at the next company.

07 · What I learned

When there is no precedent, principles do the work patterns can’t

Most product design borrows established patterns. Venmo for payments, Stripe for checkout, Linear for productivity. When you are designing for rails that do not exist anywhere else, there is nothing to copy.

Without the six principles, this product would have been re-litigated weekly.
